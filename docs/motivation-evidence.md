# Motivation & engagement evidence

Research done 2026-08-28. Companion to [`session-length-evidence.md`](session-length-evidence.md).
Written down so nobody has to search this again.

**Why this file exists:** the obvious way to make a focus timer motivating is to add
streaks, points, badges and an "hours to expert" bar. Most of that is either unsupported
or actively counterproductive, and one very common design is the single most demotivating
arrangement in the whole reward literature. This records what was checked, so the next
person doesn't rebuild the bad version.

---

## Part 1: "How many hours to become an expert?" — you cannot honestly say

### The 10,000-hour rule is Gladwell's, not Ericsson's

In the source paper (Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer 1993, *Psychological Review* 100(3)),
the string `"10,000"` appears **exactly once in 44 pages**, as a passing aside. It is never
a threshold, a rule, or a finding.

What the study actually measured — accumulated solitary practice **by age 18**, N=30:

| Group | Mean hours by 18 |
|---|---|
| "Best" violinists | **7,410** |
| "Good" violinists | 5,301 |
| Music teachers | 3,420 |
| Expert pianists (study 2) | 7,606 |
| Amateur pianists (study 2) | 1,606 |

They were **students**, and Ericsson described them as *"very good, promising students…
but they still had a long way to go"* — not experts.

Ericsson's own correction (*Peak*, 2016): *"nothing special or magical about ten thousand
hours… **Half of the ten violinists in that group hadn't actually accumulated ten thousand
hours at that age. Gladwell misunderstood this fact.**"* He also notes elite pianists
typically need 20,000–25,000 h, so the number isn't even domain-general.

### The direct replication failed

Macnamara & Maitra 2019, *Royal Society Open Science* 6:190327 (N=39) re-ran the design:

| Group | 1993 | 2019 replication |
|---|---|---|
| Best | 7,410 h | **8,224 h** (range 3,978–14,664) |
| Good | 5,301 h | **9,844 h** (range 3,120–21,268) |
| Variance explained (η²) | 0.48 | **0.26** |

The best group practised **less** than the good group (t₂₄ = −0.93, p = .364). The
original's central claim did not replicate.

### How much does practice actually explain?

Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014, *Psychological Science* 25(8) — 88 studies, N=11,135.
Overall **12% of variance** [9%, 15%]. By domain:

| Domain | Variance explained |
|---|---|
| Games | 26% |
| Music | 21% |
| Sports | 18% |
| Education | 4% |
| **Professions** | **<1%** (p = .62, not significant) |

Elite athletes specifically: **1%** (Macnamara, Moreau & Hambrick 2016).

### The finding that kills an hours counter

The same meta-analysis broke results down by **how practice was measured**:

| Measurement method | Variance explained |
|---|---|
| Retrospective interview | 20% |
| Retrospective questionnaire | 12% |
| **Log / diary (ongoing recording)** | **5%** |

Authors' words: *"for studies using the log method, which presumably yields more valid
estimates than retrospective methods do, deliberate practice accounted for only 5% of the
variance in performance."*

**This app is a log.** The more accurately you measure practice hours, the weaker the
hours→skill relationship becomes. The impressive numbers come from people *misremembering*
their practice — and Ericsson's own 1993 data shows the bias direction: self-estimates ran
**5.2 h/week higher** than diaries.

### Within-group spread makes any "% toward expert" bar false

Chess (Hambrick et al. 2014, *Intelligence* 45):

| Group | Mean | SD | Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Master (2200+), n=16 | 10,530 h | 7,414 | **832 – 24,284 h** |
| Expert (2000), n=31 | 5,673 h | 4,654 | — |

A **29× spread** to reach the same title, and **31.3% of masters had less practice than the
mean of the group one level below them.**

### Logging hours is not deliberate practice

Ericsson & Harwell 2019, *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:2396 — the three tiers:

| Tier | Requires | Can a timer do it? |
|---|---|---|
| **Deliberate practice** | Qualified teacher, individualised tasks, immediate feedback | **No** — structurally impossible |
| **Purposeful practice** | Improvement-directed solo work with a specific goal | **Partly** — only with a goal prompt |
| **Naive practice** | Merely doing the activity | **This is what a bare timer measures** |

Ericsson & Harwell, explicitly: *"nobody has argued that any single hour of practice has an
equivalent effect on improving performance."*

### Real institutional hour benchmarks (the only honest anchors)

**FSI languages** (live from state.gov; the widely-quoted 600/900/1100 figures are outdated):

| Category | Weeks | Class hours | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 24–30 | **552–690** | Spanish, French, Italian, Dutch |
| II | ~36 | **828** | German, Indonesian, Swahili |
| III | ~44 | **1,012** | Russian, Hindi, Vietnamese, Turkish |
| IV | 88 | **2,200** | Arabic, Mandarin, Japanese, Korean |

Caveats from FSI's own page: a typical week is **23 h class + 17 h self-study**, so total
time is roughly double; the target is **ILR level 3 of 5** ("General Professional
Proficiency"); and **FSI publishes no hour estimate at all for levels 4 or 5** — the
institution with the world's best hour data declines to put a number on its top two levels.

**CEFR guided learning hours** — LanguageCert per level: A1 95, A2 95, B1 180, B2 180,
C1 200, C2 250 h. Cambridge cumulative: A1 60–80 → C2 1,000–1,200 h. Both define a guided
hour as **teacher-supervised**, not solo time, and both label them guidance only.

**ABRSM music** (official Piano syllabus) — Grade 8: 54 guided hours, **320 total
qualification time**. Initial→Grade 8 sums to ~1,450 h TQT. Grade 8 is RQF Level 3, roughly
A-level equivalent — advanced amateur, **not "expert."**

**Licensure minima** (legally binding, but eligibility ≠ expertise): FAA private pilot
**40 h** (14 CFR 61.109); airline transport pilot **1,500 h** (61.159); CFA **300 h**
recommended self-study per level plus 4,000 h qualifying work. Note what medicine — the
most tightly regulated system — actually counts: ACGME general surgery requires **≥850
supervised operative cases**, not practice hours, and it *caps* hours at 80/week.

### Verdict

**No domain publishes an hours-to-expert figure.** Every real institutional number is
either instructed hours to a named mid-scale level, or a legal eligibility minimum.

What is honest:

1. Hours as a **record**, never a **predictor**: "412 hours logged on Spanish." Full stop.
2. Where a real benchmark exists, cite it **by name with its own label and caveat**, and
   say which kind of hour it counts. Let the user compare; don't compute their percentage.
3. Never map solo logged hours onto *guided* hours 1:1.
4. Replace "% toward expert" with **markers the user sets**, or with externally-assessed
   checkpoints (a graded exam, a proficiency test, a code review) — the honest signal of
   skill is assessment, not duration.

## Part 2: What actually sustains engagement

| Mechanism | Evidence | Numbers | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Progress monitoring** | Harkin et al. 2016, *Psych Bulletin* 142(2) — 138 studies, N=19,951, randomised | Goal attainment **d=0.40** [.32, .48]; stronger when physically recorded | **Strong** — best base for this app's core function |
| **Rewarding the RETURN after a lapse** | Milkman et al. 2021, *Nature* 600 — N=61,293, 54 interventions | **Best of all 54 arms**: micro-reward for returning after a missed session, **+27% weekly visits** (p<.001). Rigid-consistency arms outperformed **0%** of other conditions | **Strong** design signal |
| **Flexible > rigid** | Beshears et al. 2021, *Mgmt Sci* 67(7), N=2,508 | Flexible **+4pp**; rigid arm dropped **5pp further** post-intervention despite >2× the money | Strong |
| **One missed day is harmless** | Lally et al. 2010, *Eur J Soc Psych* 40(6), N=96 | Median **66 days** to automaticity (range 18–254). Missing a single day cut automaticity **<0.5 points**, recovered quickly | Moderate |
| **Implementation intentions** | Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer 2025 — **642 tests** | Overall d=**.36**; behaviour change **.27**; online **.31**; bias-corrected **.15**. If-then **.43** > schedule .29; rehearsal **.45** > .33; **writing it down ATTENUATES (.31 vs .44)**; 1–2 plans beat 3–5 | Strong |
| **Intrinsic → quality, incentives → quantity** | Cerasoli et al. 2014, *Psych Bulletin* 140(4), k=183, **N=212,468** | Quality: intrinsic β=**.35** vs incentives β=**.06**. Quantity: incentives β=**.33** > intrinsic .24. Intrinsic link falls ρ=.45→**.30** when incentives are directly salient | **Strong — the finding that settles the volume question** |
| **Spacing** | Cepeda 2006 (317 experiments); Baddeley & Longman 1978 (N=72) | d=0.46. Baddeley held total at 60 h: **1 h once a day was most efficient**, 2 h twice daily least | Moderate–strong |
| **Metacognitive planning + review** | Theobald 2021 — 49 studies, N=5,786 | Overall g=**.38**; **attention/concentration training g=.61** (highest) | Strong |
| **Positive informational feedback** | Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 — 128 experiments | **d = +0.33** free choice, +0.31 interest | Strong |
| **Micro-breaks** | Albulescu et al. 2022, N=2,335 | Vigour d=.36, fatigue d=.35; **performance d=.16, p=.116 — NS**; cognitively demanding **d=−0.09** | Moderate for wellbeing, **null for performance** |

### Streaks specifically

| Finding | Number |
|---|---|
| Breaking a streak hurts **~4× more** than holding one helps | Intact b=**+0.25**; broken b=**−1.01** (Silverman & Barasch 2023, *JCR* 49(6), 980 employees) |
| Streaks get gamed | GitHub **silently removed** streak counters (May 2016). RDD on **433,138 developers, 290M+ contributions**: long streaks abandoned, weekend share fell (Moldon et al. 2021) |
| Streaks induce counterproductive behaviour | RCT N=799: users with streaks highlighted made **more** attempts to open social media |
| "Streaks work because of loss aversion" | **The leading streak paper never mentions loss aversion** — its mechanism is *goal adoption*. Loss aversion as a general law is itself contested (Gal & Rucker 2018) |
| Streak freeze / grace days | **No independent research exists.** Vendor blog only |

Four independent lines converge: **forgiveness beats rigidity.** The best arm of 54
rewarded *returning*; rigid arms were duds; flexible beat rigid during and after; and one
missed day does essentially nothing to the underlying habit. A streak that resets to zero
punishes users for something the evidence says is harmless.

### The single most actionable reward finding

Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, performance-contingent breakdown (Qb(3)=25.06, p<.001):

| Reward design | Free-choice d |
|---|---|
| **Maximum** — everyone who performs gets the full reward | **−0.15** (Cameron 2001 re-analysis: **−0.03, NS**) |
| **Less-than-maximum** — "you got 3 of a possible 8" | **−0.80 to −0.88** |

DKR's words: the most detrimental design is one where *"if they do not display optimal
performance, they get smaller rewards."*

> **This condemns a very common Pomodoro-app design: "you could have earned 8 focus-coins
> today; you earned 3."** Binary "you hit your target" is roughly harmless. **Graded
> shortfall is the worst thing in the literature.** It also cautions against progress bars
> that visibly render a shortfall.

Two more DKR moderators almost nobody cites:
- **Salience**: salient reward d=**−0.78**; non-salient d=**+0.24**. Visual prominence
  alone flips the sign.
- **Scope limit**: undermining applies **only to already-interesting tasks** — "with boring
  tasks there is little or no intrinsic motivation to undermine." On low-interest tasks
  rewards *help* (Cameron, Banko & Pierce 2001: **d=+0.28**).

The two opposing camps converge quantitatively: both agree engagement-contingent tangible
rewards on interesting tasks undermine (CBP's own figure: **d=−0.35, k=57, N=2,910**),
verbal praise helps (**+0.31**), unexpected/non-contingent rewards do nothing, and harm
concentrates in the sub-maximal case.

### Gamification: a quantity lever, not a motivation lever

Mekler, Brühlmann, Tuch & Opwis 2017, *Computers in Human Behavior* 71 — 2×4 experiment
(points / levels / leaderboard / control):

- Game elements **did not significantly affect intrinsic motivation or perceived competence**
- They **did** increase output
- Authors: they *"functioned as extrinsic incentives, effective only for promoting
  performance **quantity**"*

Sailer & Homner 2020 meta-analysis: cognitive g=.49, motivational g=.36, **behavioural
g=.25** — and motivational/behavioural effects **did not hold under high methodological
rigour**.

Hanus & Fox 2015, 16-week longitudinal: the gamified course showed **less** motivation,
satisfaction and empowerment over time.

### Against leaderboards specifically

Fong, Patall, Vasquez & Stautberg 2019, *Educ Psych Review* 31(1) — 78 studies, 431 effect
sizes: negative feedback vs positive **r=−.18**. Harm is reduced when feedback is
**effectance-relevant** (tells you how to improve) and **criterion-based rather than
social-comparison-based** — and negative feedback is **more undermining when
technologically mediated** than in person. A leaderboard is technologically-mediated
normative negative feedback for everyone below the top.

### Social features are contraindicated, not merely weak

| Evidence | Result |
|---|---|
| Ntoumanis et al. 2021, 73 studies, N=30,088 | Relatedness g=0.202 NS at end, **g=0.027 NS at follow-up** |
| Gillison et al. 2019 | "Group co-operation" was a significant **negative** predictor of competence satisfaction (B=−1.52, p<.05) |
| Idrees et al. 2025 RCT, N=312, 8 weeks | Week-8 active participation: **control 7.62%**, gamification 3.85%, **social support 2.86%** — both intervention arms retained *fewer* users |

### Self-determination theory — weaker than its reputation

| Evidence | Result |
|---|---|
| Sheeran et al. 2020, *JCCP* 88(8) — **RCTs only**, 65 tests | **d=0.23**; publication bias confirmed (p=.002); **trim-and-fill d=0.15**. Authors: *"SDT interventions are not very powerful"* — from interventions averaging **9.52 hours of human contact** |
| **Altendorf et al. 2019, N=521** — clean 2×2 of autonomy-supportive language × choice in a *digital* intervention | **ALL HYPOTHESES REJECTED** (language p=.799; choice p=.921; interaction p=.584) |
| **Linardon et al. 2025, *npj Digital Medicine* — 92 RCTs, N=16,728** | Apps work (g=0.43), but **number of persuasive-design principles predicted neither efficacy (b=0.01, p=.804) nor engagement (r=0.21, p=.43)** |

What survives at feature level: **giving a rationale** (B=1.07, p<.001), **non-controlling
language** (B=1.86, p<.05), and **offering choices** — largest for trivially "irrelevant"
ones like themes and sounds (Patall 2008, 41 studies, r=.15), with the moderator that
choice's benefit **shrinks when an external reward is also present**.

## Myths — do not ship these

1. **"10,000 hours makes you an expert."** Gladwell's phrase. Appears once, incidentally, in
   the source paper. Ericsson publicly repudiated it.
2. **"Deliberate practice explains most of expert performance."** 12% overall; <1% in
   professions; 1% among elite athletes; **5% when measured by log** — which is what an app does.
3. **"Logging focused hours is deliberate practice."** It is *naive* practice by Ericsson's
   own final taxonomy.
4. **"FSI says 600/900/1100/2200 hours."** Outdated. Current: 552–690 / 828 / 1,012 / 2,200,
   and they are *classroom* hours plus 17 h/week separate self-study.
5. **"Streaks work because of loss aversion."** The leading streak paper never mentions it;
   the mechanism is goal adoption, and loss aversion itself is contested.
6. **"21 days to form a habit."** Maxwell Maltz, *Psycho-Cybernetics* (1960) — a plastic
   surgeon's observation about patients adjusting to a new self-image. Never a habit study.
   Real figure: median **66 days**, range 18–254.
7. **"Implementation intentions have d=.65."** Obsolete. The 2025 update over 642 tests gives
   **.36** overall, **.27** for behaviour change, **.15** after bias correction.
8. **"Have users write down and save their plan."** Contradicted — recording it
   **attenuates** the effect (.31 vs .44). If-then phrasing and rehearsal are the levers.
9. **"Variable rewards make apps addictive" (Eyal, *Hooked*).** Practitioner synthesis, never
   tested as a causal model of app engagement. The neuroscience it borrows (Schultz's reward
   prediction error — solid) doesn't support the leap, and dopamine ≠ pleasure (Berridge's
   wanting/liking dissociation). The nearest real evidence base is loot-box research, which
   is **correlational harm evidence**. **No A/B test or experiment shows variable rewards
   beat fixed rewards for sustained engagement in a non-gambling productivity app.**
10. **"Every distraction costs 23 minutes."** The published figure is **25 min 26 s, SD 54 min
    48 s** (Mark, González & Harris, CHI '05, N=24) — 23:15 appears in no peer-reviewed
    source. It measures wall-clock time to return to a project-level *working sphere*,
    **includes 2.26 other pieces of real work done in between**, and its SD is more than
    double its mean. Worse for the myth: Mark, Gudith & Klocke (CHI '08) found interrupted
    subjects finished **faster** (20.31/20.60 vs 22.77 min, p<.05) with no more errors — the
    real cost was **stress, frustration and effort**. Pitch focus protection as stress
    reduction, not time recovery.
11. **"Amabile's progress principle is strong evidence."** Real design (238 professionals,
    ~12,000 diary entries) but **within-person correlational diary data**, and mood and
    perceived progress are self-reported in the same instrument on the same day. The
    circulating 76%/67% figures could not be verified. **Use Harkin et al. 2016 (d=0.40)
    instead — same product implication, far better evidence.**
12. **"Small rewards are better than none."** Gneezy & Rustichini 2000, *QJE* 115(3): small
    monetary incentives produced **worse** performance than no incentive at all.

## The volume tension

The daily budget feature is **better supported than any hours milestone would be — and by
the very paper the 10,000-hour rule came from.** Ericsson et al. 1993:

> *"These studies show essentially no benefit from durations exceeding 4 hr per day and
> reduced benefits from practice exceeding 2 hr."*

The best violinist groups practised **3.5 h/day** in ~90-minute sessions and slept **8.6 h**
(vs 7.8 h for the least accomplished).

**Honest caveat, in keeping with this project's no-folklore policy:** that 4-hour claim is
itself only **moderately** supported. Its citations in Ericsson 1993 are **Welford (1968)
and Woodworth & Schlosberg (1954)** — a narrative review pointing at mid-century secondary
texts. The modern long-hours literature (Pencavel) concerns ~50 h/week in manual and
clerical work, a different construct at a different scale. Label it moderate, not strong —
but it is still the best-evidenced number in this product.

**Why rewarding volume is the wrong lever:**

1. **Spacing is the killer argument.** Baddeley & Longman held total training at 60 hours and
   varied only the schedule: **1 h once a day beat 2 h twice a day.** Same hours, more skill.
   A cumulative-hours counter rewards the *worse* schedule.
2. **Cerasoli quantifies the cost.** Incentives predict quantity; intrinsic motivation
   predicts quality; and intrinsic motivation's contribution *shrinks* when incentives are
   tied directly to the metric.
3. **Quantification itself is a risk.** Etkin 2016, *JCR* 42(6), six experiments: measuring
   an activity makes people do **more** of it and **enjoy it less**, which then reduces
   continued engagement. This app is a measurement device — measure lightly, and never make
   the number the point.
4. **Rigidity doesn't even work.** Megastudy arms rewarding consistent schedules
   outperformed **0%** of the other 53 conditions.

## What this app does as a result

Built 2026-08-28:

| Feature | Mechanism | Grounds |
|---|---|---|
| **Session intention** — one if-then prompt, **once a day**, text box optional | Moves the app from naive toward *purposeful* practice | 1–2 plans > 3–5; if-then .43 > schedule .29; rehearsal .45; **writing it down attenuates**, hence optional |
| **Session review** — Scattered / Steady / Deep, no score | Informational feedback, not a reward | Feedback **d=+0.33**; tangible completion rewards **d=−0.36** |
| **"Days practised · last 28"** replacing the day-streak counter — never resets to zero | Forgiving consistency | Broken streak b=−1.01 vs intact +0.25; one miss is harmless (Lally); GitHub removed streaks |
| **Comeback note** after a gap ≥2 days, framed as spent not owed; fresh-start wording on a new week/month | Rewarding the return | **Best of 54 arms, +27%** (N=61,293); Dai/Milkman/Riis fresh-start effect |
| **Hours logged per skill**, with a per-skill **hour goal** and a log-scaled bar filling toward it | Progress monitoring; goal-gradient | Monitoring **d=0.40**; goal-gradient is **weak** evidence (Kivetz N=108, t=2.0, unreplicated) but costs nothing here |
| **Daily budget bar** turning `--good` at target | A ceiling, not a target | Ericsson 1993 (moderate) |

### Two decisions that went against this file, on purpose

**The bar fills toward the goal.** It was first built sized against the largest skill,
explicitly to avoid rendering a shortfall. That was over-applying the reward research:
the **d=−0.80 to −0.88** finding is about a *reward* scaled to how far short you fell
("you earned 3 of a possible 8"), not about a self-set goal with nothing riding on it.
Progress monitoring (**d=0.40**) is the better-evidenced mechanism and it argues for
showing the progress. The original design also made length and colour mean two different
things: the biggest skill sat permanently full while the colour still flipped on reaching
the goal, so changing that goal appeared to do nothing.

**The default goal is 10,000 hours**, set by the project owner. This file documents at
length why no hours-to-expert threshold is defensible, and a default is the app
*suggesting* a number rather than the user choosing one — so this is a deliberate
departure, recorded here rather than quietly contradicted. The number is one constant,
`DEFAULT_SKILL_GOAL_HOURS` in `js/app.js`.

**The bar is log-scaled** (`goalProgressPct`): `log(hours+1) / log(goal+1)`. Two failed
attempts preceded it, and both failures are instructive:

1. *Linear to the goal.* Correct but useless at the default: under about 100 hours of a
   10,000 h goal the bar sat at 0-1% and never appeared to move.
2. *Linear to the next rung of a milestone ladder* (`SKILL_MILESTONES`: 10, 50, 100, 500,
   1000, 2500, 5000, 10000, capped by the goal). The bar moved, but it had stopped
   depending on the goal: the rung only reflected the goal when the goal was set *below*
   it, so 45 h logged read 90% whether the goal was 50 h or 10,000 h. Re-setting the goal
   changed nothing, which is worse than a bar that barely moves — a control that visibly
   does nothing reads as broken.

A log scale is the only one satisfying both constraints at once. It is strictly decreasing
in the goal (raising the goal always shortens the bar) and strictly increasing in hours
(the bar never drops back — no rung-crossing regression). It is also the honest shape for
what is being measured: under the power law of practice, 10 h → 20 h is comparable progress
to 1000 h → 2000 h. The cost is that 50% no longer means half the hours, so the tooltip
carries the raw figures.

The ladder is kept, demoted to the tooltip's "next 500h" signpost — a concrete next step
for someone who cannot estimate how many hours a skill needs. Green and the ✓ still
require the **goal**, so "reached" keeps one meaning.

Deliberately **not** built:

- Any label claiming a number of hours makes you an expert — no domain supports it
- Points, coins, badges (**d=−0.36**), or any graded "3 of 8" shortfall (**d≈−0.85**)
- Variable/random rewards — no supporting evidence; nearest evidence base is harm research
- Leaderboards or social/community features — actively contraindicated (see above)
- Cumulative hours as the hero number — rewards the schedule that teaches less
- Notification nagging — would be shipping on lore

Design constraints applied throughout: **keep reward signals visually non-salient**
(salient d=−0.78 vs non-salient +0.24), and **ship few mechanics, not many** (feature count
predicted neither efficacy nor engagement across 92 RCTs, N=16,728).

## Genuine gaps — do not ship on these

- **Notification volume / fatigue / uninstall behaviour** — no solid evidence located.
- **Streak freeze / grace-day efficacy** — vendor and practitioner reports only.
- **Loss-framed pressure causing abandonment** — no direct harm evidence. Note the
  asymmetry: the *justification* for loss framing (loss aversion) is contested, and the
  megastudy arm that let users **choose** gain vs loss framing worked (+19%). The defensible
  position is "let the user pick," not "loss framing is harmful."
- **Etkin 2016 effect sizes** — the six experiments and the qualitative pattern are solid;
  no effect size should be attached.
- **Lepper, Greene & Nisbett 1973** — the circulating "8.59% vs 16.73%" and "N=51" are
  secondary-source only. A same-lab near-replication (N=73) does exist: only **55%** of
  Expected-Award children showed post-test interest vs **86%** in the other groups
  (χ²=8.16, p<.01). Never subjected to an adversarial multi-lab replication.

**Overarching caution.** In the megastudy, impartial expert judges **failed to predict**
which of 54 interventions would work, and only **8%** produced effects measurable after
four weeks. The literature says which mechanics are *defensible*; it cannot say which will
work here. Instrument and measure.
